LEADERSHIP INTERVIEW — Ironwood Door Co. — Derek Vance, Owner / General Manager — 2026-02-11

1. In one paragraph: what does this business do, and for whom?
We repair and install residential garage doors and openers for homeowners across the East Valley — Mesa, Gilbert, Chandler, Tempe, some Scottsdale. My dad started it in '98. We fix broken springs, cables, openers, and we sell and install new doors when someone's ready to upgrade. It's a service business first, but installs are where the real growth is if we can figure out how to sell more of them.

2. Who are your 2–3 real customer segments? Which one pays the bills?
There's the panic homeowner — door's broken, they need it fixed today, don't care much about price. There's the planner — thinking about a new door for curb appeal or a smart opener, shopping around. And there's builders/property managers who need volume work. Repair pays the bills day to day, no question. Installs are the segment I want to grow because the margin's better and it's less "emergency room," more "consultation."

3. Why do customers pick you over the alternative?
Brand recognition, honestly. We've been around almost thirty years, my dad's name is on half the trucks in Mesa still. People remember the yellow trucks. I think if we invested more in marketing — better website, more ads, maybe some TV or radio — we'd convert a lot more of the install-curious people who don't already know us.

4. How does money actually come in? (streams, rough % each)
Repair calls are probably 65% of revenue — springs, openers, tune-ups. Installs are maybe 25%. The rest, call it 10%, is maintenance plans and small parts/accessory stuff. I'd like to flip that ratio somewhat toward installs over the next couple years.

5. What 3 resources/capabilities could you not operate without?
Our tech team — some of these guys have been with us fifteen, twenty years, they're fast and they don't make people feel ripped off. Our parts inventory and supplier relationships, because same-day repair only works if the truck's stocked. And honestly our name — "Ironwood" still means something in this valley.

6. Who are your critical partners/suppliers?
LiftMaster and Chamberlain for openers, Clopay for doors, a couple regional spring and hardware distributors out of Phoenix. We've got decent terms with all of them because we've been a customer for so long.

7. What is the single biggest thing limiting growth right now?
Marketing spend. We're basically living off referrals and repeat business from thirty years of goodwill, and that only gets you so far. We're not showing up enough online, we don't run real ad campaigns, our website's dated. If I had a bigger budget for lead gen I think installs would take off.

8. What are the 12-month goals, in priority order?
One, grow install revenue — I want it at 35% of the mix by end of year. Two, get a real marketing budget in place, even if it means trimming somewhere else. Three, maybe add a second install crew if the demand's there. Four, get our online reviews and presence looking sharper — we've got great reviews but they're scattered across sites nobody checks.

9. What is broken internally that everyone knows and nobody fixes?
Our install sales process is too slow — we don't get quotes out fast enough, and I think that costs us deals to guys who show up same day with a number. That's on me to fix, probably need a dedicated estimator instead of pulling install crew off jobs to go quote.

10. If a competitor wanted to kill you, what would they attack?
Our slow install turnaround and the fact that our digital presence is weak compared to the newer companies. Someone with a slicker website, faster online booking, and aggressive ads could out-market us with a fraction of our reputation.

LEADERSHIP INTERVIEW — Ironwood Door Co. — Carla Nunez, Office Manager — 2026-02-11

1. In one paragraph: what does this business do, and for whom?
We're the people you call when your garage door won't open and you've got to get to work, or your kid's trapped in the car in July. Mostly repairs — springs, openers, cables. We also do new door installs but that's a smaller slice. Our customers are homeowners all over the East Valley, a lot of them repeat customers or people whose neighbor gave them our number.

2. Who are your 2–3 real customer segments? Which one pays the bills?
Emergency repair customers, no contest — that's most of what rings my phone. Then there's the maintenance-plan folks who call once a year like clockwork, bless them. Installs are a small, slower-moving group. Repair is what keeps the lights on, easily 70% of what actually gets scheduled and invoiced in a given week.

3. Why do customers pick you over the alternative?
Because when they call, a tech shows up and fixes it right, same day usually, and doesn't try to upsell them into something they don't need. That's it. That's the whole reputation — word of mouth from people who had a good experience. Marketing doesn't build that. Thirty years of not screwing people over built that.

4. How does money actually come in? (streams, rough % each)
Repair calls, I'd say 70%. Install jobs, maybe 20%, and honestly that number should be higher given how many install-curious calls I field and can't get scheduled fast. Maintenance plans and small parts, 10%.

5. What 3 resources/capabilities could you not operate without?
The techs — full stop, they're the whole business. The dispatch board, which is basically me and a whiteboard and a scheduling app I don't fully trust. And the parts we keep on hand, because if a truck isn't stocked right the whole day's schedule falls apart.

6. Who are your critical partners/suppliers?
LiftMaster, Chamberlain, Clopay — same as everyone in this business. The relationship that actually matters day to day is with our local spring and hardware distributor, because if they're out of a part on a Friday, that's a customer waiting till Monday.

7. What is the single biggest thing limiting growth right now?
We can't answer the phones fast enough. I'm not exaggerating — during peak season, mornings and after storms, calls go to voicemail because I'm already on another line or scheduling a truck. After 5pm and weekends it's worse, it's just voicemail and a callback the next morning, and by then half those people already called someone else. We are turning away business we already have, before we even get to marketing more of it.

8. What are the 12-month goals, in priority order?
One, get us actual after-hours phone coverage — even an answering service would help. Two, hire another CSR so calls aren't stacking up on me during peak hours. Three, get scheduling off my whiteboard and into a system the techs can see themselves. Four, sure, grow installs, but only after we stop leaking the repair calls we already have.

9. What is broken internally that everyone knows and nobody fixes?
I own the phones, the scheduling, the permit filings, payroll, and half the HR paperwork. If I'm out sick or slammed, dispatch just doesn't happen right — jobs get double-booked or missed entirely. Everybody knows this is too much on one person. Nobody's fixed it because fixing it costs money and Derek keeps saying we need to spend on ads first.

10. If a competitor wanted to kill you, what would they attack?
Our phones. Any competitor with a real answering system, online booking, or after-hours coverage could catch every call we drop. We're sitting on decades of goodwill and just... not answering the phone sometimes. That's the whole vulnerability right there.
