LEADERSHIP INTERVIEW — Quartermast — Dana Reyes, Founder & CEO — 2026-02-11

1. In one paragraph: what does this business do, and for whom?
We give small field-service companies — plumbing, electrical, garage doors, that kind of trade — one place to schedule jobs, dispatch techs, and get paid, instead of a whiteboard and a shared calendar. My brother ran a plumbing company for twelve years on a paper board and I watched him lose jobs because two guys got sent to the same address. That's who we build for: the owner who's still doing dispatch themselves at 6am with a coffee and a Sharpie.

2. Who are your 2–3 real customer segments? Which one pays the bills?
Plumbers, electricians, and garage door companies, roughly in that order of volume. Plumbers pay the bills — they're our biggest segment by revenue and they expand seats fastest once they're in, because a plumbing company adding a truck adds a tech who needs a login the same week.

3. Why do customers pick you over the alternative?
Price and simplicity. The big guys — the ServiceTitans of the world — are built for 50-truck operations with a full-time office admin running the software. Our buyer doesn't have that person. They pick us because a tech can learn it in twenty minutes and it doesn't cost what a truck payment costs.

4. How does money actually come in? (streams, rough % each)
About 90% is subscription — per-seat monthly, techs plus a base platform fee. The other 10% is a one-time setup fee we started charging this year because implementation was eating our support team alive and free wasn't filtering out the people who weren't serious.

5. What 3 resources/capabilities could you not operate without?
The scheduling engine itself — that's the actual product, everything else is wrapper. Our relationship with the trade associations we sponsor, which is basically our whole marketing funnel. And honestly, me — I still close every deal, so whatever it is in a sales call that gets someone from "interesting" to "signed," that's a resource right now, even if it shouldn't be long-term.

6. Who are your critical partners/suppliers?
QuickBooks, for the invoicing sync — a huge chunk of our prospects ask about it in the first five minutes of a call. Twilio for SMS job reminders. And a couple of regional trade associations that let us sponsor their conferences, which is where most of our leads come from.

7. What is the single biggest thing limiting growth right now?
Lead volume. I close basically every deal I get in front of, our win rate on qualified calls is strong, so the ceiling is how many of those calls we get. We need more top-of-funnel — more marketing spend, more conference presence, more inbound. If I had twice the leads I'd have twice the ARR by next year.

8. What are the 12-month goals, in priority order?
One, get to $3M ARR. Two, hire a second closer so I'm not the bottleneck on every deal — though honestly I'm not sure anyone else can sell it like I can, these owners want to talk to the founder. Three, ship a native payments feature so we stop losing deals on "does it just charge the card." Four, expand past the three verticals we're in now.

9. What is broken internally that everyone knows and nobody fixes?
Onboarding is ad hoc. Whoever on the CS team has a free slot that week runs the new customer's setup call, and there's no real checklist, so quality varies a lot. I know it's a problem. It hasn't felt urgent because our churn numbers look okay in month one — it's more of an efficiency thing than a growth blocker, I think.

10. If a competitor wanted to kill you, what would they attack?
Our depth. We're wide across three trades but not that deep in any one of them — a competitor who went all-in on just garage door companies and built every feature those guys specifically want could out-execute us in that niche. That's the risk I actually lose sleep over.

LEADERSHIP INTERVIEW — Quartermast — Priya Chandran, Head of Customer Success — 2026-02-13

1. In one paragraph: what does this business do, and for whom?
We're the system a small trade business runs its day out of — who's going where, what job they're on, whether the invoice went out. It's for owners who've outgrown a paper board but are nowhere near ready for the enterprise field-service platforms. The honest version is: we're for people who are terrified of software and need it to just work on day one, because they don't have a second chance to convince their techs to use it.

2. Who are your 2–3 real customer segments? Which one pays the bills?
Plumbers, electricians, garage door companies. I'd actually say garage door companies pay the bills in the way that matters — they renew the longest and they don't nickel-and-dime us on seats. Plumbers bring in the most revenue up front because they're the biggest segment, but they also churn the hardest in month three or four, so a lot of that revenue doesn't stick.

3. Why do customers pick you over the alternative?
Dana would say price and simplicity, and that's true for why they sign. But why they actually pick us — versus just staying on a spreadsheet forever — is that in the sales call, Dana makes them a promise: "we'll get you off paper painlessly." The people who stay are the ones where we actually deliver on that promise during onboarding. The people who churn are the ones where we didn't.

4. How does money actually come in? (streams, rough % each)
Subscription, almost all of it — I don't track the split precisely, that's more a Dana/finance thing, but it's overwhelmingly recurring seats. What I watch is net revenue after the month-three cliff, and that number is quietly getting worse each cohort.

5. What 3 resources/capabilities could you not operate without?
Our data migration tooling — even the rough version we have — because it's the difference between a customer who trusts us and one who doesn't. Our two CS reps, who are stretched way too thin. And the mobile app, because if a tech won't open it on the truck the whole thing falls apart regardless of what the owner signed up for.

6. Who are your critical partners/suppliers?
QuickBooks, definitely — but I'd flag that our QuickBooks sync is one-way and shallow, and I hear about it on churn calls more than Dana probably realizes. Twilio for reminders. And honestly, whatever spreadsheet or paper system the customer is migrating FROM is a kind of hidden partner — how clean their old data is decides how hard the first thirty days will be.

7. What is the single biggest thing limiting growth right now?
Activation, not leads. We get customers in the door fine. What kills us is that a meaningful chunk of them never actually migrate their job history — old jobs, old customer notes, service history — into Quartermast. So by month three they're running half their business in the new system and half in their head or an old spreadsheet, it feels like double the work, and they leave. More leads just means more people falling into that same hole.

8. What are the 12-month goals, in priority order?
One, fix onboarding — give it an actual owner, an actual checklist, a real migration path, before we add another dollar of ad spend chasing new logos. Two, get the QuickBooks sync deeper so it's not a lost-deal reason anymore. Three, hire a dedicated onboarding specialist — right now it's whoever's free, which means it's nobody's real job. Four, everything Dana wants, which I mostly agree with, just after the leaks are patched.

9. What is broken internally that everyone knows and nobody fixes?
Onboarding, full stop. Everyone agrees it's inconsistent. But because our month-one churn looks fine, it never gets prioritized over sales and product asks — the pain shows up two months later, on my team's desk, in a churn call, where it's much harder to trace back to "the setup call in week one went badly."

10. If a competitor wanted to kill you, what would they attack?
Onboarding, same as my answer to nine, honestly. If a competitor showed up with a real white-glove migration — someone who actually moves your job history for you instead of asking the owner to do it themselves — they'd take our accounts in month three without even trying that hard on features.
